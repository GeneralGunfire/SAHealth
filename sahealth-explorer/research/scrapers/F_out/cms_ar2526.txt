CMS ANNUAL REPORT 2025/26
Introduction
The healthcare sector remains a critical pillar of South 
Africa’s social and economic development. Access to 
quality, affordable healthcare not only improves health 
outcomes but also contributes to the well-being, 
productivity, and resilience of communities. Within this 
context, medical schemes and their stakeholders continue 
to play an important role in extending access to healthcare 
for millions of South Africans.
As the regulator of the medical schemes industry, the 
Council for Medical Schemes (CMS) remains committed 
to protecting the interests of members and beneficiaries. 
It is dedicated to promoting a healthcare funding 
environment that is fair, transparent, sustainable, and 
responsive to the needs of those it serves. During the 
year under review, the industry continued to demonstrate 
resilience despite affordability pressures, rising healthcare 
costs, increasing utilisation demands, and broader 
economic challenges affecting households across the 
country.
The CMS oversees 70 medical schemes, comprising 16 
open and 54 restricted schemes, covering approximately 
9.17 million beneficiaries. These beneficiaries are served 
by 36 administrators, 42 managed care organisations, 7 
793 accredited individual brokers and 2 182 accredited 
brokerages. Despite a challenging operating environment, 
the industry remains financially sound, recording a 
solvency ratio of 40.87% in 2024, significantly above the 
statutory minimum requirement of 25%.
The 2025/26 financial year was characterised by significant 
developments in healthcare policy and regulation. These 
included continued progress towards health system 
reform; important milestones in implementing the 
recommendations of the Section 59 Investigation; 
advancement of the Fraud, Waste, Abuse and Error (FWAE) 
framework, and ongoing efforts aimed at strengthening 
affordability, transparency, and accountability within the 
medical schemes environment.
Equally important was the continued strengthening and 
stabilisation of the CMS as an institution. The Council 
remained focused on sound governance, organisational 
resilience, regulatory effectiveness, and strategic alignment 
during the first year of implementing the CMS’ 2025–2030 
Strategic Plan. Collectively, these developments reflect an 
organisation and an industry that continue to evolve in 
response to changing expectations of fairness, value, and 
member protection.
Overview of the Strategy and 
Performance of CMS
The year under review marked the first year of 
implementing the CMS’ 2025–2030 Strategic Plan. 
Guided by its vision of becoming an efficient, effective, 
high-quality, agile, and trusted regulator generating 
sustainable stakeholder value, the CMS continued to 
strengthen its regulatory capability, governance systems, 
and stakeholder engagement efforts.
The organisation achieved an overall performance score 
of 94.83% against its planned targets. This performance 
reflects the commitment of Council, Management, and 
employees to delivering on the CMS’ mandate while 
remaining responsive to the needs of members, 
beneficiaries, and industry stakeholders.
The CMS continued to strengthen beneficiary protection 
through its complaints adjudication function, one of the 
most visible expressions of the organisation’s mandate. 
During the year under review, the organisation processed 
10 818 written submissions through its complaints

channels. It also responded to 8 363 enquiries and 
registered 2 455 formal complaints, representing a 25.1% 
increase compared to the previous financial year. Together 
with matters carried forward from the previous year, the 
CMS investigated a total caseload of 2 911 complaints. 
These efforts demonstrate continued public confidence 
in the CMS as an independent avenue for dispute 
resolution and member protection.
The organisation also continued to strengthen regulatory 
oversight, compliance monitoring, governance, and 
policy capability. Significant progress was made in: 
implementing the recommendations of the Section 59 
Investigation Final Report; advancing the FWAE agenda; 
progressing the review of Prescribed Minimum Benefits 
(PMBs), and strengthening consumer education and 
empowerment initiatives.
The CMS remains committed to sound governance, 
accountability and prudent financial management. During 
the year under review, Council continued to strengthen 
oversight mechanisms, internal controls and governance 
processes to support the effective execution of the 
organisation’s mandate. I am pleased to report that the 
CMS attained a clean audit outcome for the 2025/26 
financial year, reflecting the organisation’s continued 
commitment to good governance, financial discipline, 
transparency and accountability in the management of 
public resources.
The medical schemes industry continued to demonstrate 
resilience despite economic pressures and rising 
healthcare costs. Beneficiary numbers increased from 
approximately 9.13 million in 2023 to 9.17 million in 
2024. While this growth is encouraging, it remains below 
broader population growth. The industry continues to 
face demographic shifts, with an ageing beneficiary 
population contributing to increased healthcare utilisation 
and expenditure.
The total benefits paid increased by 8.52% to R259.3 
billion in 2024, while claims paid per beneficiary increased 
by 7.84%. The PMB expenditure increased by 9.09% to 
R135.66 billion and out-of-pocket expenditure reached 
R46.3 billion. These trends reinforce the importance of 
ongoing efforts to improve affordability, strengthen 
financial protection, and ensure the long-term 
sustainability of healthcare funding arrangements.
Strategic Relationships
The CMS recognises that meaningful and sustainable 
reform can only be achieved through collaboration. 
During the year under review, the organisation continued 
to strengthen its relationships with various role players. 
These included the Ministry of Health; the National 
Department of Health (NDoH); regulatory authorities; 
healthcare practitioners; medical schemes; administrators, 
and other key stakeholders in support of its regulatory 
mandate and broader healthcare reform objectives.
Particular emphasis was placed on dialogue and 
consultation relating to the implementation of the 
recommendations of the Section 59 Investigation Final 
Report. Through ongoing engagements with healthcare 
practitioners, professional associations, medical schemes, 
and administrators, the CMS sought to ensure that 
implementation processes remain balanced, transparent, 
and responsive to stakeholder concerns.

The CMS also provided transitional guidance to the 
industry while broader legislative and policy reforms 
continue to be explored.
The CMS further supported the work of advisory and 
collaborative structures established to advance key 
regulatory priorities. These included stakeholder 
platforms relating to the Fraud, Waste, Abuse and Error 
(FWAE) framework, as well as advisory structures 
supporting the review of Prescribed Minimum Benefits 
(PMBs). The engagements with these platforms and 
structures continue to play an important role in ensuring 
that regulatory interventions are informed by technical 
expertise, stakeholder perspectives, and evidence-based 
policy considerations.
The CMS Industry Indaba represented an important 
milestone in strengthening dialogue, trust, and 
cooperation across the healthcare funding sector. The 
platform enabled regulators, medical schemes, 
administrators, healthcare practitioners, policymakers, 
and other stakeholders to engage constructively. They 
deliberated on matters relating to the implementation of 
the Section 59 recommendations, the future direction of 
the FWAE framework, healthcare affordability, value-
based care, and the broader health system reform. 
Among the key outcomes was stakeholder support for 
the inclusion of  ‘Error’ within the FWAE framework. This 
recognised the need for a more balanced and 
proportionate approach to addressing irregular claims 
and payment practices across the healthcare funding 
environment.
The CMS also strengthened its commitment to evidence-
based regulation through the Research Symposium, 
which brought together researchers, policymakers, 
regulators, and industry stakeholders to engage on 
emerging healthcare funding issues. The Symposium 
forms part of a broader strategy aimed at strengthening 
the use of research and evidence in regulatory decision-
making and policy development. This commitment was 
further reinforced through ongoing work towards 
establishing a peer-reviewed journal focused on medical 
schemes, healthcare financing, and health policy research.
Strategic partnerships remain an important enabler of the 
CMS’ work. 
During the year under review, the organisation maintained 
and concluded five Memoranda of Understanding, 
exceeding its annual target. These partnerships support 
coordinated approaches to regulation, research, capacity 
building, stakeholder engagement, and the resolution of 
systemic challenges within the healthcare funding 
environment.
The CMS also continued to strengthen its longstanding 
partnership with the Committee of Insurance, Securities 
and Non-Bank Financial Authorities (CISNA). The latter 
provides an important platform for regional collaboration, 
knowledge sharing, and engagement on social health

Legislative and Other Mandates 
Legislative mandates
Section 9 of the Constitution of the Republic of South 
Africa (108 of 1996) states that everyone has the right to 
equality, including access to health care services. This 
means that individuals should not be unfairly excluded 
from the provision of health care.
People also have the right to access information that is 
held by another person if it is required for the exercise 
or protection of a right. This may arise in relation to 
accessing one’s medical records from a health facility for 
the purposes of lodging a complaint or for giving 
consent for medical treatment. This enables people to 
exercise their autonomy in decisions related to their 
health, which is an integral part of the rights to human 
dignity and bodily integrity in sections 9 and 12 of the 
Constitution, respectively.
Section 27 of the Constitution places the obligation on 
the State to put reasonable legislation in place to realise 
socio-economic rights, including access to health care 
progressively.
The Medical Schemes Act (131 of 1998) (MSA) represents 
such legislation, which creates the framework for non-
discriminatory access to medical schemes. The MSA 
provides for the regulation of the medical schemes 
industry to ensure synchrony and consonance with the 
national health objectives.
Section 27 of Chapter 2 of the Bill of Rights of the 
Constitution states the following with regards to health 
care, food, water, and social security:
Everyone has the right to access to:
• Healthcare services, including reproductive healthcare.
• Sufficient food and water.
• Social security, including appropriate social assistance, 
if they are unable to support themselves and their 
dependents.
The State must provide reasonable legislative and other 
measures within its available resources to achieve the 
progressive realisation of these rights, and no one may 
be refused emergency medical treatment.
Section 36 of the Constitution deals with the limitation 
of rights and it spells out strict criteria which must be 
adhered to whenever the rights included in the Bill of 
Rights are limited by law. Section 22 of the Constitution 
guarantees the freedom of trade, which may be limited 
by law.
The Medical Schemes Act limits the business of a 
medical scheme to those parties registered by the 
Council for Medical Schemes and requires such parties 
to comply with the provision of the Medical Schemes 
Act.
The National Health Act (61 of 2003) (NHA)
The NHA provides the framework for a structured 
uniform health system for our country, considering the 
obligations imposed by the Constitution and other laws 
on the national, provincial, and local governments 
regarding health services. A key objective of the NHA is 
to unite the various elements of the national health 
system to actively promote and improve the  system in 
South Africa. Added to this is the intent to foster a spirit 
of cooperation and shared responsibility among all role 
players. These include public and private health 
professionals, providers, and other relevant stakeholders 
within the context of national, provincial, and district 
health plans.
The Charter for the Public and Private 
Health Sectors of South Africa, 2006
This Health Charter was initiated in support of the NHA. 
It indicates that the public and private health sectors 
need to constructively engage with each other in 
discussions and dialogue to create an improved 
healthcare delivery system for South Africa. Such a 
system will need to be coherent, efficient, cost-effective 
and quality-driven, and optimise the use of both sectors’ 
resources to benefit the entire citizenry.
The Medical Schemes Act (131 of 1998)
The Medical Schemes Act (131 of 1998) established the 
Council for Medical Schemes (CMS). Section 7 of the 
MSA confers the following functions on the CMS:
• Protect the interests of the beneficiaries at all times.
• Control and coordinate the functioning of medical 
schemes in a manner that is complementary to the 
national health policy.
• Make recommendations to the Minister of Health on 
criteria for the measurement of quality and outcomes 
of the relevant health services provided by medical  
schemes. Also make recommendations on such other 
services as the Council may from time to time  
determine.
• Investigate complaints and settle disputes in relation 
to the affairs of medical schemes as provided for in  
this Act.
• Collect and disseminate information about private 
health care.
• Make rules not inconsistent with the provisions of the 
Act for the purpose of the performance of its functions 
and the exercise of its powers.
• Advise the Minister of Health on any matter concerning 
medical schemes.
• Perform any other functions conferred on the CMS 
by the Minister of Health or the Act.

Healthcare Expenditure and Benefits Paid
Healthcare expenditure increased between 2023 and 
2024. The total benefits paid rose from R239.0 billion in 
2023 to R259.3 billion in 2024, an 8.52% year-on-year 
increase. This growth rate exceeded both general inflation 
and average wage growth, reinforcing the structural 
mismatch between healthcare cost escalation and 
household affordability. On a per-beneficiary basis, claims 
paid per average beneficiary per annum (PABPA) 
increased by 7.84%, rising from R26 404.69 in 2023 to 
R28 474.15 in 2024. Risk benefits paid per beneficiary 
increased even more sharply, by 8.71%, indicating 
growing pressure on pooled funds. The distribution of

years. Hospital services accounted for 35.95% of total

supplementary and allied health professionals accounting

cost pressures are systemic rather than driven by isolated 
service categories.

Out-of-pocket payments continued to rise between 2023 
and 2024, reflecting both higher utilisation and persistent 
gaps between provider charges and scheme 
reimbursements. At an industry-wide level, total out-of-
pocket expenditure increased at an average annual rate 
of 6.09% over the long term, reaching R46.3 billion in 
2024. Between 2023 and 2024, medicines dispensed 
remained the largest contributor to out-of-pocket costs, 
accounting for 35.09%, while payments to specialists 
increased from 27.59% to 28.47%, reflecting a 0.88 
percentage point rise. Although restricted schemes 
continued to exhibit lower out-of-pocket costs, both 
open and restricted schemes experienced growth in 
member-paid costs, reinforcing concerns about the 
erosion of financial protection.

Shift to Value-Based Healthcare
Globally, there is a clear shift towards Value-Based Health 
Care (VBHC), which prioritises improving healthcare 
quality and patient outcomes over merely increasing the 
volume of services. For medical schemes, this involves 
adopting payment models that reward providers for 
delivering care that demonstrably improves patient 
outcomes, such as outcomes-based or results-oriented 
care. However, transitioning to VBHC presents several 
challenges. The traditional Fee-for-Service (FFS) model 
incentivises high service volumes, which can result in 
over-treatment, higher costs, and a focus away from 
patient outcomes. Bundled payments, intended to 
control costs, require precise risk adjustment to avoid 
under-treatment, particularly for patients with complex 
medical conditions. Capitation models carry financial risk 
if actual care costs exceed fixed payments, potentially 
encouraging providers to restrict the necessary care.
Effective VBHC implementation also depends on robust 
data systems to measure performance accurately, while 
providers must adapt to new care models, which can be 
resource-intensive and operationally complex. In the 
South African context, developing a national framework, 
standards, and industry guidance for VBHC is critical. 
Such guidance would support medical schemes and 
providers in implementing outcomes-focused care. It 
would also ensure consistent and equitable practice, and 
use data-driven insights to improve quality, enhance 
health equity, and strengthen access to high-quality care 
across all populations.

National Health Insurance
The National Health Insurance (NHI) represents a 
transformative health financing system. It is designed to 
pool public resources into a single NHI Fund to purchase 
quality, affordable personal health services for all South 
Africans according to their needs, advancing Universal 
Health Coverage (UHC). The Fund will contract directly 
with public and private providers, allocating resources to 
hospitals, primary healthcare facilities, and emergency 
services. This will take place through global budgets, 
capitation, or case-based fees, with services determined 
by the Benefits Advisory Committee. Key components, 
including a single-payer system, standardised benefits, 
the integration of public and private providers, and a 
reduced role for medical schemes, are intended to 
broaden coverage. It is also intended to reduce inequities 
and ensure lower point-of-service costs. However, these 
reforms have significant implications for medical schemes. 
This includes a reduced operational role, potential 
membership and funding shifts, and the need to realign 
benefit packages to complement the NHI Basic Benefits 
Package. Schemes must also adopt governance, 
contracting, and claims processes to interface with the 
NHI Fund. This highlights the importance of proactive 
regulatory oversight and strategic adaptation to maintain 
financial sustainability and relevance in a transformed 
health system. Policy implications (also depicted in Figure 
8 below) include the need for legislation and regulatory 
frameworks that support scheme adaptation, protect 
consumer interests, and maintain social solidarity. At the 
same time, it must  ensure that private sector participation 
strengthens rather than undermines the objectives of 
UHC.
Due to this evolving landscape, the CMS, mandated 
under section 7(b) of the Medical Schemes Act (131 of 
1998), plays a pivotal regulatory and advisory role. The 
CMS ensures that schemes comply with governance, risk 
management, and consumer protection standards while 
facilitating their alignment with NHI objectives. This 
includes harmonising Prescribed Minimum Benefits with 
the NHI Basic Benefits Package and integrating primary 
healthcare coverage. It also includes advising on benefit 
structures, contribution models, and contracting 
arrangements, and participating in technical NHI 
committees. The CMS also supports the NDoH by 
providing technical expertise on scheme market 
structures, funding mechanisms, and operational 
performance. To mitigate the NHI-related challenges, the 
CMS has incorporated the NHI provisions into its Strategic 
Plan 2025–2030. This enables schemes to adapt while 
upholding social solidarity principles and protecting 
beneficiaries. Its strategy combines supportive oversight, 
evidence-based guidance, strengthened governance, 
and transparent communication to maintain stakeholder 
confidence. It ensures that medical schemes continue to 
operate sustainably and contribute effectively to the 
national health system while integrating seamlessly with 
NHI objectives. From the policy perspective, this 
underscores the critical need for coordinated regulation, 
clear guidance on complementary coverage, and 
mechanisms that incentivise private sector alignment with 
national health priorities.

Geopolitical Tensions
Import Dependency
The first challenge that the CMS has to face is the issue of 
import dependency. South Africa remains reliant on 
imported pharmaceuticals, medical devices, diagnostic 
equipment, and specialised healthcare consumables. 
Escalating geopolitical tensions between Iran and the 
United States have the potential to disrupt global trade 
routes and shipping networks. This is a particular threat if 
instability affects the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world’s 
most critical maritime trade routes for oil and international 
shipping. Such disruptions can increase shipping times, 
freight costs, and supply chain uncertainty, resulting in 
longer procurement lead times and a reduced availability 
of essential medical supplies. Healthcare providers may 
also face delays in obtaining critical medicines, laboratory 
reagents, surgical equipment, and other products 
required for uninterrupted patient care.
Increased Costs
Another challenging issue is an increase in costs across 
various sectors. Geopolitical instability can contribute to 
higher global energy prices, increased freight and 
logistics costs, and broader inflationary pressures. 
Disruptions to oil exports through the Strait of Hormuz 
may result in higher fuel prices, increasing the cost of 
manufacturing, transporting, and importing medical 
products and healthcare consumables. Rising procurement 
costs place additional pressure on healthcare budgets 
and may contribute to higher medical scheme premiums 
and increased out-of-pocket healthcare costs for patients. 
Inflationary pressures may also affect operational 
expenses, including utilities, transport, maintenance, and 
outsourced services, reducing the purchasing power of 
healthcare institutions.
Service Delivery Environment
The service delivery environment is also prone to 
challenges. Supply chain disruptions and inflationary 
pressures may adversely affect the continuity and 
efficiency of healthcare service delivery. Delays in the 
availability of essential medicines, medical devices, and 
consumables could impact treatment schedules, elective 
procedures, and overall patient care. To mitigate these 
risks, the organisation continues to strengthen supply 
chain resilience through supplier diversification, strategic 
inventory management, an ongoing monitoring of 
geopolitical developments, and business continuity 
planning. These measures support the continued delivery 
of safe, reliable, and quality healthcare services despite 
an increasingly uncertain global operating environment.
Economic Outlook
The general economic outlook remains bleak due to 
various challenges. Persistent geopolitical tensions may 
weaken the global economic growth, reduce investor 
confidence, and contribute to exchange rate volatility. 
For South Africa, these developments could result in 
sustained inflation, higher import costs, and slower 
economic growth, placing additional pressure on the 
healthcare sector. Continued uncertainty may affect both 
public healthcare funding and private sector affordability, 
reinforcing the need for prudent financial management, 
operational efficiency, and proactive risk mitigation.
Life Expectancy and Health Expenditure in 
South Africa
Life expectancy and health expenditure are further issues 
of importance. South Africa’s health outcomes continued 
to improve modestly during 2024, despite persistent 
economic and healthcare system challenges. According 
to Macro-trends, life expectancy at birth increased from 
64.88 years in 2023 to 65.10 years in 2024. This reflects 
ongoing improvements in access to healthcare services, 
HIV/AIDS treatment programmes, maternal and child 
healthcare, and the management of communicable and 
non-communicable diseases.
South Africa continues to allocate a relatively high 
proportion of its GDP to healthcare when compared  to 
many upper-middle-income countries. However, higher 
levels of health expenditure have not translated into life 
expectancy outcomes comparable with those of several 
peer economies. This reflects the influence of broader 
structural factors, including socioeconomic inequality, 
disparities in access to quality healthcare, the dual public-
private healthcare system, the burden of disease, and 
inefficiencies within the health system.
For the medical schemes industry, these trends highlight 
the importance of improving healthcare efficiency, 
promoting preventive care, strengthening access to 
quality healthcare services, and ensuring the sustainability 
of healthcare financing. Continued collaboration between 
regulators, healthcare providers, medical schemes, and 
policymakers remains essential to improving health 
outcomes while maintaining affordable and equitable 
access to healthcare.

Due to all these challenges, the CMS is implementing 
various policy reforms. The central aim of the policy 
reforms provided for in the MSA is to enhance the risk 
pooling potential of medical schemes and strengthen 
important regulatory and oversight mechanisms by 
implementing the following reforms: 
• A preferred health insurance vehicle, which requires 
that any person doing the business of a medical scheme 
operate in terms of a single legislative framework. 
• Open enrolment, which removed the discriminatory 
practice of medical schemes to select only good-risk  
beneficiaries for membership (risk selection). 
• Mandatory minimum benefits, which removed the 
ability of schemes to discriminate against older and   
sicker members through the selective non-provision 
of key benefits. 
• Waiting periods and late joiner penalties: to eliminate 
any significant application of penalties regarding 
member movement between medical schemes and 
options while substantially removing the opportunities 
for anti-selection where a member joins only when sick 
and then leaves or only joins for the first time later in 
life. 
• Improved governance, which removed the historical  
conflicts of interest embedded in the oversight of 
medical schemes. 
• Regulation of intermediaries, which implemented 
accreditation and more stringent regulatory oversight 
of medical scheme brokers, administrators, and 
managed care organisations. 
• Member protection, which includes the complaints 
resolution mechanisms at the scheme level and provides 
members access to the complaints’ resolution 
mechanisms at the Registrar’s office and appeals 
processes. These reforms remain relevant to this day. 
The original intentions behind the  introduction of

funders operate on a level playing field. It should 
maximise the advantages and minimise the

multi-fund health industry. However, many facets of

certificate applications. As of 31 March 2026, there were 14 accredited administrators; 9 limited services 
administrators; 13 medical schemes with administration compliance certificates; 35 accredited managed care 
organisations and 7 medical schemes with managed care compliance certificates. Performance under Output 18 
(Medical scheme rules comply with the Medical Schemes Act) was largely achieved.
Output Indicator 18.1 (percentage of rule amendments processed within 30 working days) exceeded the planned 
target of 80%, achieving 88.9% (48/54). This represents a +8.9% positive deviation. This is attributable to improved 
workflow management, enhanced internal performance monitoring, and the implementation of structured processes 
that ensure faster turnaround times once complete submissions are received. The integration of technical and 
administrative functions further reduced processing delays and improved responsiveness to stakeholders.
Output Indicator 18.2 (percentage of annual rule amendments processed before 31 December) achieved 100% 
(69/69) against a target of 95%, reflecting a 5% positive deviation. This outcome demonstrates high levels of 
regulatory efficiency and discipline, supported by early stakeholder engagement, strengthened internal planning, 
and proactive follow-up mechanisms. These measures ensured that submissions were complete and compliant at 
the point of review, allowing for uninterrupted processing and timely approvals of benefit and contribution changes. 
This  is critical for scheme sustainability and member protection. 
Output Indicator 18.3 (the publication of Contributions and Benefits Changes Guidance Notes) remained stable, 
with the annual target achieved. This continues to support regulatory certainty and consistency in how medical 
schemes design contribution increases and benefit changes, reinforcing compliance with legislative and policy 
requirements.
Output Indicator 18.4 (the review and publication of Model Rules) was not achieved, with no outputs recorded 
against the planned target. The delay is attributable to the need for additional legal review and quality assurance 
processes. The purpose is to ensure that the Model Rules are fully compliant with the Medical Schemes Act and 
are legally sound and implementable. While this resulted in a short-term performance gap, it reflects a deliberate 
prioritisation of regulatory integrity over speed, given the foundational importance of Model Rules in guiding 
scheme governance and member relations.

An annual assessment of medical schemes with fewer than 6 000 principal members found that 30 small risk pool 
schemes remained in operation. This represents 42% of registered medical schemes but only 2.11% of industry 
beneficiaries. While these schemes maintained solvency levels above the statutory minimum, the assessment found 
that they experienced higher claims volatility (16.73% compared to 9.45% for larger schemes). They also had older 
and higher-risk membership profiles, and higher administrative costs. The findings provide evidence to support the 
strategic amalgamation of small schemes into larger, diversified risk pools. The purpose will be to improve financial 
resilience, reduce administrative costs, strengthen risk pooling, and support the long-term sustainability of the 
private healthcare financing sector.

Programme 2: Regulation
The purpose of the Programme is to: 
• Ensure that brokers and broker organisations, administrators, and managed care organisations are accredited in 
line with the accreditation requirements as set out in the Medical Schemes Act (131  of 1998). This includes whether 
applicants are fit and proper, have the necessary resources, skills, capacity and infrastructure, and are financially 
sound. 
• To serve beneficiaries of medical schemes and the public in general by reviewing and approving changes to   
contributions paid by members and benefits offered by schemes. The Programme analyses and approves all scheme 
rules to ensure consistency with the MSA. This ensures that the beneficiaries have access to affordable and appropriate 
quality health care. By doing this, we help the CMS ensure that the rules of medical schemes are fair to beneficiaries 
and are consistent with the MSA. 
• Serve members of medical schemes and the public in general by taking appropriate action to enforce compliance  
with the Medical Schemes Act. 
• To monitor and regulate the overall financial performance of medical schemes in line with the financial requirements 
of the MSA, through the analysis of financial information.

Strategy to overcome areas of under-performance
Benefits Management
As far as benefits management is concerned, the sub-
programme is responsible for the processing of scheme 
rules, the monitoring of marketing material, and the 
provision of guidance on contribution increases and 
benefit changes. These functions contribute to the 
achievement of the CMS’ mandate of regulating medical 
schemes in accordance with the MSA and protecting the 
interests of beneficiaries.
Medical scheme operations, including contributions, 
benefits, and governance arrangements, are governed 
by registered rules. The sub-programme therefore plays 
a critical role in ensuring that rule amendments are 
consistent with the MSA and support fair and equitable 
access to benefits.
During the 2025/26 financial year, the sub-programme 
achieved and exceeded most of its planned targets.
The target relating to the review and publication of Model 
Rules was not achieved due to extended legal and quality 
assurance processes required to ensure alignment with 
the Medical Schemes Act and regulatory requirements.
To address the under-performance, the CMS will allocate 
dedicated legal and technical resources, introduce a 
milestone-driven review process and initiate stakeholder 
engagement earlier. Quality assurance will also be 
integrated earlier in the drafting process, supported by 
enhanced monitoring against clearly defined timelines, to 
minimise delays and reduce rework.

Introduction
The Council is appointed by the Minister of Health and is 
responsible for the strategic oversight of the entity. It 
approves the 5-Year Strategic Plan, the Annual 
Performance Plans, the Internal Control Framework as 
well as the policies of the organisation. The Council 
operates on the basis of an approved Charter. All six 
committees have approved Charters. 
The Charters complement the existing legislative 
instruments such as the: 
• Medical Schemes Act (131 of 1998) (MSA)
• Public Finance Management Act (1 of 1991) (PFMA) 
• National Treasury Regulations
• Inspections of Financial Institutions Act (80 of 1998)
Further, the Council draws its governance philosophy 
from the King IV Code of Corporate Governance in South 
Africa. The Council will, during the course of 2026 be 
trained in the new code, King V.  It views governance to 
embody such principles and practices as ethical 
leadership, effective control, monitoring and evaluation, 
and ethical culture.
Portfolio Committees 
During the reporting period, the CMS engaged with the 
Portfolio Committee on Health on key governance 
matters. The Annual Report for 2024/25 was presented 
on 9 October 2025, and the Annual Performance Plan 
(APP) for 2025/26 was presented in January 2026 and was 
approved. The Council met with the Portfolio Committee 
on the following dates:
• 23 April 2025 
• 9 October 2025
• 25 March 2026
• 30 April 2026
Executive Authority 
In terms of the PFMA, the Council is required to submit 
quarterly reports to the Executive Authority. These reports 
range from financial management, performance 
information, and strategic risk management, to 
compliance. All reports were submitted timeously during 
the reporting period as follows as per the requirements of 
the PFMA:
• Quarter 1 – 31 July 2025
• Quarter 2 – 31 October 2025
• Quarter 3 – 31 January 2026
• Quarter 4 – 30 April 2026
There were no issues of concern raised by the Executive 
Authority regarding the reports submitted by the Council. 
The Accounting Authority
The accounting authority of the CMS is the Council. The 
Minister of Health appoints up to 15 members to the 
Council, selected from diverse sectors of society and 
bringing expertise in areas such as accounting, economics, 
medicine, law, and civil society. In terms of the MSA, the 
Council is responsible for:
• Protecting the beneficiaries of medical schemes.
• Controlling and coordinating the functioning of   
 medical schemes.
• Advising the Minister of Health on the quality and   
 outcomes of relevant health services provided by   
 medical schemes.
• Investigating complaints and resolving disputes, and
• Collecting and disseminating information about the   
 private healthcare industry.
Beyond its statutory responsibilities, the Council also 
carries out its broader governance oversight function by:
• Evaluating and approving the five-year Strategic Plan.
• Evaluating and approving the Annual Performance   
 Plan. 
• Evaluating and approving the annual financial   
 statements and annual performance information   
 report.
• Exercising oversight of the executive management’s   
 performance.
The Council exercises its functions in terms of the Medical 
Schemes Act, the Public Finance Management Act, 
Treasury regulations, other applicable laws, and its charter 
and code of conduct. Further, its work is carried out by 
various committees that report directly to it. The Chief 
Executive Officer (CEO) and Registrar is accountable to 
the Council for his duties.
Council Charter
The Council has adopted a formal Charter to regulate its 
conduct. The Charter outlines its responsibilities for 
approving strategy, monitoring executive management 
performance, ensuring effective risk management and 
internal controls, and overseeing the implementation of 
approved policies. 
The Council’s Charter is reviewed on an annual basis 
together with the Committee’s Charters. The Council is 
satisfied that it has complied with its Charter. The recent 
Council evaluation conducted by an external independent 
service provider gave the Council a score of more than 
80% in terms of its performance as a Council. This is 
testimony to its comprehensive Charter that is strictly 
complied with at all times.

Business Continuity Management 
During the period, the CMS established a consistent  
Business Continuity Management (BCM) process, 
covering both the development and execution of plans. 
The aim of BCM is to strengthen resilience in the day-to-
day business operations, but also to ensure the effective 
management of unexpected disruptive events. Business 
disruptions can affect any organisation and arises from 
factors such as adverse weather, political unrest, hazardous 
spillages, power outages, or pandemics.
To mitigate these risks the CMS has developed Business 
Continuity Plans (BCPs) and Business Impact Analysis 
(BIAs). The BIAs and BCPs have been reviewed annually, 
or as circumstances require. In addition, the CMS has 
conducted the penetration testing and vulnerability 
assessment during the prior year and is busy preparing 
for the cyber risk management maturity assessment for 
this financial year. Continuous disaster recovery testing 
has been conducted to ensure business continuity, 
prevent devastating financial losses, and minimise 
prolonged downtime. 
The CMS has integrated its BCM policy, ICT backups and 
disaster recovery policy.  There is a BCM committee in 
place to ensure that:
• There is a developed, reviewed and implemented BCM 
framework in place.
• Critical business functions and associated risks are

Framework for Addressing Breaches of the 
Code of Conduct
The CMS has established a clear and structured 
disciplinary framework to address breaches of the Code 
of Ethics and Conduct. The framework distinguishes 
between less serious and serious offences, ensuring 
proportionality, fairness, and due process in managing 
misconduct.
Less Serious Offences
When a breach is deemed to be of a less serious nature, 
the CMS management initiates an informal disciplinary 
hearing. The following key features apply:
• The process typically involves only the employee 
concerned and their immediate supervisor or manager.
• The objective is to address the issue swiftly, promote 
corrective behaviour, and prevent recurrence without 
the need for formal sanctions.
• Possible outcomes include a verbal or a written 
warning, depending on the severity and circumstances 
of the breach.
• Documentation of the warning is maintained for 
reference but is generally less formal than a full 
disciplinary record.
This informal approach encourages open communication, 
remediation, and an early resolution of minor breaches.
Serious Offences
For breaches classified as serious, the CMS applies a 
more formal disciplinary process to uphold organisational 
standards and accountability. This process includes:
• Convening a formal disciplinary hearing, convened 
by a chairperson appointed by the Registrar.
• Appointment of an initiator who presents the case 
on  behalf of the CMS, ensuring that the facts and  
evidence are clearly articulated.
• The accused employee’s right to representation by a 
fellow CMS employee or a recognised shop steward.
The employee is entitled to:
• Present their case and provide evidence in their 
defence. Cross-examine witnesses who testify against 
them.
• Make submissions in mitigation before any sanction is 
imposed.
• Potential sanctions for a guilty verdict include a final  
written warning, suspension without pay, or 
dismissal,  depending on the gravity of the offence 
and any mitigating or aggravating factors.
This formal disciplinary procedure is conducted in strict 
adherence to principles of natural justice, ensuring that 
the employee receives a fair hearing and that decisions 
are based on an objective assessment of facts.
Governance and Oversight
The disciplinary framework forms a critical component of 
the CMS’ broader governance and ethics programme. 
The Registrar, supported by the HR and legal units, 
oversees the implementation of disciplinary processes to 
ensure consistency and compliance with labour 
legislation and the CMS’ policy. Outcomes of disciplinary 
hearings, particularly those involving serious breaches, 
are reported to the ARC and the Accounting Authority as 
part of the organisation’s commitment to transparency 
and accountability.
Continuous Ethics Development
To strengthen and sustain a culture of ethical conduct, 
the CMS complements its disciplinary framework with a 
range of preventative and developmental ethics 
initiatives. These initiatives are designed not only to 
promote awareness but also to equip employees with 
the practical tools necessary to recognise, address, and 
appropriately respond to ethical challenges in the 
workplace.
As part of these efforts, the CMS provides staff with the 
CMS Ethics Booklet, which outlines expected standards 
of conduct, practical guidance on ethical decision-
making, and procedures for reporting misconduct. In 
addition, the CMS regularly facilitates ethics workshops 
and training sessions, tailored to promote an 
understanding of the Code of Ethics and Conduct and its 
application to real-world scenarios. These sessions 
encourage open dialogue, clarify ethical expectations, 
and support employees in aligning their behaviour with 
the CMS’ values.
During the period under review, the CMS also undertook 
a formal Ethics Assessment Exercise to evaluate the 
current ethical climate within the organisation. The 
assessment aimed to identify strengths, areas for

These initiatives reflect the CMS’ ongoing commitment
