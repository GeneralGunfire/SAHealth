Message on overall audit outcomes
Overall audit outcome
Current culture
• Overall, there was a regression in the sector outcomes due to GP receiving a qualified audit opinion 
compared to unqualified opinion in the prior year. The regression resulted in five out of the 10 (50%) 
departments in the sector now having qualifications.
• There was no improvement from any of the other departments compared to the prior year audit 
outcomes. NC reported a reduction in the number of qualification paragraphs.
• The prevailing culture across these provinces appears to be one of weak accountability, inadequate 
ownership of controls, limited consequence for non-performance, and a reactive approach to 
governance, resulting in recurring audit findings, repeat qualifications and an inability to achieve 
sustainable improvements in audit outcomes.
Root causes
• Weak accountability for resolving repeat findings and implementing corrective actions.
• Poor stewardship of financial, asset and performance information, resulting in recurring misstatements 
and unreliable reporting.
• Reactive oversight, with risks addressed during audits rather than through ongoing monitoring and 
intervention
Recommendations
• Enforce accountability through measurable commitments, regular monitoring and consequence 
management.
• Strengthen preventative controls over records management, asset management and financial 
reporting.
• Focus oversight on implementation of corrective actions and resolution of root causes rather than 
action-plan tracking.

Fiscal pressure – Medico-legal claims
What we found
• Apart from the Western Cape, Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal, no 
provinces made specific budgetary provisions for medico-legal 
claims in the 2025-26 financial year. As a result, the settlement of 
these claims continues to place significant fiscal pressure on the 
health sector, diverting limited resources away from strategic 
priorities and service delivery objectives.
• The financial impact of medico-legal claims is particularly evident at 
facility level, where funds earmarked for goods and services, 
medical supplies, infrastructure maintenance and other operational 
requirements are often reallocated to settle litigation claims. 
• This has a direct and adverse effect on the ability of departments to 
deliver quality healthcare services, as resources intended to support 
patient care are used to meet legal obligations.
Recommendation
• Medical-legal claims are an inherent risk within the operations of 
the health sector. However, leadership should prioritise addressing 
the shortage of medical personnel to improve service delivery, 
enhance patient outcomes, and reduce the likelihood of adverse 
incidents that may result in claims.
• Departments should strengthen internal controls over claims 
management, particularly in relation to the maintenance, 
safeguarding and accessibility of patient medical records. 
Adequate record-keeping is critical to supporting the defence of 
claims and reducing exposure to litigation losses.
• Departments should implement robust processes to independently 
assess and regularly review the valuation of medico-legal claims. 
Reliance solely on the amounts stated in summonses may result in 
significant fluctuations and inaccuracies in the reported liability.

Root causes:
• Weak SCM and procurement processes, controls, oversight 
and monitoring.
• Limited storage capacity and incomplete archiving; poor 
linen tracking records.
• Budget constraints, centralised recruitment delays and weak 
workforce planning.
• Theatre shortages and cancellations; limited awareness of 
PSI targets and governance requirements.
Impact:
• IPC measures, patient safety and emergency preparedness 
are compromised.
• Medical supplies, clean linen and complete patient records

• Strengthen SCM and procurement, stock control and 
oversight in wards.
• Secure compliant records storage and tighten tracking of 
patient records.
• Improve planning, resourcing and filling of critical posts.
• Offices of the HoD and MEC to comply with governance 
structure requirements of the National Health Act.

Regional hospitals (follow-up)
Some improvements were noted however the following repeat findings were noted:
• Stock control remains weak, with medical supplies, consumables and equipment not consistently available in

• Hand hygiene facilities were missing or not functional (5 provinces).

• Repeat findings on stock control, hand hygiene, HR vacancies, supplies and records storage recur, although 
some improvement is evident (6 provinces).
Tertiary and central hospitals
• Stock control remains weak, with medical supplies, consumables and equipment not consistently available in 
wards (all 8 provinces).
• Hand hygiene facilities were missing or not functional (8 provinces) and linen and laundry services did not 
comply with standards (7 provinces).
• HR remains inadequate: critical posts vacant and scarce orthopaedic skills hard to recruit (8 provinces).
• Emergency trolleys were not re-stocked (7 provinces) and water supply was not checked daily (3 provinces).
• Surgical backlogs and long waiting times persist, worsened by theatre cancellations (8 provinces); one fully 
equipped ward remained unused (1 province).
• Governance structures were absent, incomplete or not functioning (8 provinces), with slow quality improvement 
plans (3 provinces), infrastructure defects (4 provinces) and inadequate patient safety incident management (3 
provinces).

District hospital services
Audit scope :
• District hospital services delivery audit at 40 district hospitals in five provinces (EC: 
16, FS: 13, MP: 4, NC: 4, NW: 3)
• Focus: provision of the district hospital package of care, availability of a 
functional operating theatre and human resources
Root causes:
• There is no functional operating theatre, which limits surgical and maternity services.
• Existing theatre structures cannot be operationalised due to a lack of trained 
doctors, staff shortages and infrastructure challenges.
• Centralised recruitment processes cause long turnaround times in appointing staff.
Impact:
• Patients travel farther and at greater cost to access basic surgical and maternity 
services.
• The next level of care is overcrowded by referrals that could be treated locally.
• Delayed procedures increase the risk of patient safety incidents and poor health 
outcomes.
Actions/Recommendations:
• Develop a service delivery plan detailing how existing operating theatres in these 
district hospitals will progressively be operationalised.
• Conduct a staffing needs analysis for the provision of district hospital services.
• Plan and budget appropriately based on that analysis.

Infrastructure delivery and maintenance
Audit scope :
• Infrastructure investment drives economic growth, job creation and service 
delivery, but its benefits depend on projects being completed on time, within 
budget, to quality standards and sustainably maintained.
• A total of 27 of 28 infrastructure projects were audited (17 follow-up projects) 
and 20 hospitals were visited for condition assessment (11 regional, seven 
tertiary, two central).
Root causes:
• Poor upfront planning, incomplete designs and undefined scope.
• Weak contract administration and procurement irregularities in needs assessment, 
bid evaluation and verification.
• Late payments that disrupt contractor cash flow.
• Overreliance on implementing agents and slow decisions on non-performing 
contractors.
Impact:
• Delayed payment certificates caused contractor delays, disputes, site 
abandonment and terminations.
• Project delays, cost escalations, poor workmanship and poor value for money.
• Late land or servitude approvals force redesigns mid construction.
• Departments that cede responsibility to implementing agents cannot intervene 
when performance declines, and termination decisions lag, sometimes over a 
year.
Actions/Recommendations:
• Strengthen upfront planning, feasibility and readiness assessments before 
procurement.
• Improve funding, commitment and cash-flow planning to avoid stoppages.
• Strengthen contract administration, oversight of implementing agents and timely 
action on non-performance.
• Enforce consequences, recall guarantees and fund and monitor maintenance.

Information technology (IT) environment 18
Overall status:
• The status of IT controls inthe health sector remains unchanged compared to prior years.
• The audit highlighted recurring challenges in ICT governance and processes, ICT investment, infrastructure and connectivity, system implementation 
and utilisation, as well as project and contract management.
What we found
• The National Digital Health Strategy (NDHS), concluded in 
2024 with minimal impact and is under review.
• Minimal value realized from IT projects of newly developed 
systems.
• Insufficient connectivity to enable usage of the service 
delivery systems.
• Digitization of patient records projects face delays, quality, 
security, and oversight challenges.
• Pharmaceutical legacy systems have limitations that hinder 
improvement of pharmacy operations.
• Underutilisation of key systems with system not delivering on 
the intended value.
Causes of findings:
• Lack of governance structures and processes to drive the implementation 
of the NDHS, oversee IT investments, rollout of IT projects and contract 
management practices.
• Lack of consequence management as the Accounting Officers do 
not ensure that a governance structures are established and are effective 
in their duties.
• Misalignment of requirements, roles and responsibilities between NDoH and 
provincial departments.
Impact:
• Incomplete projects resulting in systems not being used and not providing 
expected benefits resulting in operational inefficiencies and hindering 
improvement on service delivery.
• Ineffective implementation and use of systems results in the continued 
reliance on manual processes, leads to inefficiencies and a higher 
likelihood of human errors, which increase the risk of inaccuracies in 
inventory management, patient records and reporting.
• Insufficient connectivity hinders the effective use of service delivery 
systems, impacting the quality and efficiency of healthcare services.

Overall message to portfolio committee
Portfolio committee message – overall audit outcomes and service delivery
Root causes, recommendations and Overall message
Key root causes
• Management does not consistently take ownership of addressing control deficiencies, implementing audit action plans, 
and holding officials accountable for results. As a result, findings recur year after year despite recommendations and 
agreed actions.
• Departments do not consistently maintain reliable records, perform adequate reviews, reconcile information, or ensure the 
integrity of financial and performance information. This results in material misstatements, poor decision-making information, 
and audit qualifications.
• Management often responds to issues only after audit findings are raised rather than identifying and addressing risks 
through ongoing monitoring and oversight. This prevents sustainable improvement and allows non-compliance and 
control weaknesses to become institutionalised.
Key 
recommendations
1
20
• Assign executive ownership for every repeat finding, establish quarterly accountability reviews by the HOD and Audit Committee, 
and implement consequence management where corrective actions are not delivered within agreed timelines.
• Implement mandatory quarterly quality reviews of financial, performance and compliance information supported by 
independent assurance functions (internal audit, risk management and finance) before year-end reporting.
• Embed controls into daily operations, link management performance agreements to audit outcomes and implementation of 
corrective actions, and focus leadership discussions on root causes and service delivery risks rather than audit findings alone.
Overall message
• The sector must continue to advocate for the inclusion of the ideal hospital status indicator in the APP for the remaining provinces 
to ensure that it will be reported, monitored and supported by the  relevant province.
• The accounting officer must ensure that the maintenance of existing facilities is prioritised within the budgetary framework and that 
appropriate measures are implemented to safeguard these assets against theft and vandalism.
• The sector's challenge is not knowing what needs to be fixed, but consistently implementing and sustaining corrective action. Until 
accountability, control discipline and proactive oversight improve, repeat findings and qualifications will persist
